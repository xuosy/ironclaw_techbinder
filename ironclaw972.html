How do we start CAD?

Computer-Aided Design

Parts are built fast, tested immediately, and changes are made on the spot to see what works and what doesn't. The pieces aren't perfect — they're made of crude objects like wood and duct tape. However, this quick approach helps us understand real-world performance versus numbers, refine mechanisms, and solve obvious problems early. This saves time, money, and much effort later through the season.

Once a design proves effective, we transfer it into CAD for precise modeling and further prototyping.

What does the CAD process look like?

Once a prototype is proven, we recreate it in CAD to build a precise and fully defined model. We refine dimensions, improve geometry, and ensure all components fit together correctly. This acts as a framework for the overall robot design, allowing us to visualize how everything fits and make adjustments before manufacturing.

  • Rapid physical prototyping with wood and duct tape before committing to CAD
  • Full robot layout assembly to verify fit and catch interference early
  • 3D printed parts for camera mounting and custom components
  • Drivetrain CAD modeled specifically for intentional wire routing and CAN organization
  • Continuous evolution — quick CAD adjustments as competition season progresses

Idea to Iteration

Manufacturing

Iron Claw 972 begins every project with an idea, which we refine and develop into a detailed design and prototype. Through iteration and ideating, we turn concepts into real and functional parts. We rigorously test each component, checking performance and continually making improvements to ensure everything works as efficiently and reliably as possible. Our team takes ideas from concept all the way to field-ready systems.

Design to manufacturing

Before manufacturing begins, our team uses CAD software to model each part and subsystem in detail. This allows us to visualize how components will fit together, check clearances, and identify potential issues early. We iterate on designs quickly, making adjustments based on testing, feedback, and performance goals. By fully developing our ideas in CAD first, we ensure our parts are precise, efficient, and ready for manufacturing.

How do we make our parts?

Once designs are finalized, our team moves into manufacturing, where digital models are turned into physical parts. Using a combination of manual tools and fabrication techniques, we produce components with quality. Some parts are simulated using CAM software, which generates the toolpaths and instructions needed to machine the part accurately. The finalized program is uploaded to our CNC machine. All round objects or shafts are done on a lathe and most flat objects are cut or CNC'd.

  • CAM-generated toolpaths for CNC machine precision
  • Lathes for all round objects and shafts
  • Band saws and mills for flat profiles and structural parts
  • Tight loop between prototype → test → CAD → machine → repeat
  • All subsystems designed for 15-minute field serviceability

Hardware to Software

Software & Vision

The hardware setup prioritizes performance and reliability. Each pair of cameras connects to an Orange Pi 5, allowing for fast onboard processing of vision data. A Redux Voltage Regulator ensures stable power delivery, protecting sensitive electronics with over-voltage, under-voltage, and over-current safeguards. The cameras are 100fps global shutter modules, which reduce motion blur for consistent AprilTag detection during fast robot movement.

Swerve & motion control

The robot uses WPILib swerve libraries controlling four independently rotating modules for field-centric driving. Each module uses PID feedback with feedforward (kS, kV, kA). CTRE Phoenix odometry threading runs at high frequency, improving position tracking during fast motion. A modified Team 254 SwerveSetpointGenerator limits acceleration to reduce wheel slip and prevent tipping.

Vision & pose fusion

PhotonVision generates pose via multi-tag pnp on coprocessor, with lowest-ambiguity single-tag as fallback. WPILib's SwerveDrivePoseEstimator fuses vision with drivetrain odometry via Kalman filter. When slip is detected at ≈0.3m, standard deviations increase to trust vision more heavily.

Driver assists

  • Trench assist: aligns to nearest 180°, zeroes Y-axis, aggressive PID to thread trench smoothly
  • Passive alignment: correction speed added to driver input based on target pose deviation
  • Score on the Move: 0.3s phase-shift prediction accounts for robot velocity at shot time
  • Auto hood retract approaching trench · X-wheel defensive lock · Alliance-aware controls

Planning First

Electrical & Control Systems

Assembly is closely coordinated with wiring for efficiency and accessibility throughout the build process. Rather than completing wiring after the robot is fully assembled, electrical work is done alongside assembly. This allows the control systems to be routed properly before spaces become difficult to reach and prevents issues caused by blocked access points.

Planning ahead is critical, as poor timing or coordination can lead to longer wire lengths, messy routing, unsecured connections, and an increased risk of failure. By coordinating assembly and control systems, we avoid problems that arise when trying to wire a fully built robot.

Electrical & wiring

During assembly, wiring is done to maintain precision and organization. Wires are connected on one end, routed along their intended path, and then cut to the exact required length before final connection. This minimizes waste and ensures clean, secure layouts. Fully wiring a competition robot can take many hours, especially in dense areas like the main control board.

Working in parallel

By coordinating assembly and control systems, 972 ensures that components remain easily accessible, wiring paths are clean, and potential issues are caught early. This reduces the need to disassemble parts later, saving time and preventing possible damage. Parallel work also allows faster iteration as mechanical and electrical systems can be changed together.

  • Inverted CSB layout since 2023 — maximizes top-deck robot space for subsystems
  • Strain relief via ziptie mounts near all connector ports — reduces wire movement and failure risk
  • 3 isolated CAN buses: drivetrain CANivore, subsystem CANivore, RoboRio loop for LEDs
  • Power poles on all motors · Molex connectors replacing SM connectors
  • Every drivetrain wire secured to restrict movement without crushing insulation
  • Weights: CSB+wiring 17.1 lb · Bumpers 13.9 lb · Drivetrain 28.4 lb

REBUILT — Game Overview

In REBUILT presented by Haas, two competing alliances score fuel, cross obstacles, and climb the tower. Alliance hubs dynamically shift between active and inactive based on autonomous performance, rewarding the team that wins auto.

Autonomous · 20s
Robots autonomously score fuel into their hub without driver input. Fuel can be pre-loaded, obtained from human players, collected at the depot, or picked up throughout the center of the field. Winning auto activates the opposing alliance's hub, giving a strategic fuel-shuttling advantage in the teleop period.
TeleOp · 2m 20s
Drivers control robots to score fuel while hubs alternate active/inactive based on auto outcome. Robots shuttle fuel from the neutral zone during inactive periods. 972 scores on the move from anywhere in the alliance zone at 8 BPS with full vision assist — the driver doesn't need to look at the hub.
Endgame — Climb
All hubs become active as time runs out, allowing all robots to score. Robots climb the tower for additional points and match bonuses. 972 typically avoids auto-climbing to maximize fuel scoring — climbing only when acting as the weaker alliance member to clear the way for stronger scorers.
